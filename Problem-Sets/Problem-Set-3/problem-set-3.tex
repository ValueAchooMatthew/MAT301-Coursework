\begin{subproblem}
	Let $G$ be a group of order 27. Prove that $G$ has an element of order $3$.
\end{subproblem}

\begin{solution}
	To prove that $G$ has an element of order 3, we need to demonstrate that there exists some element $x \in G$ such that $n = 3$ is the smallest positive integer satisfying $x^{n} = 1$. \\

	So to that end, let's let $a \in G \setminus \set{1}$ be an arbitrary element in $G$ (besides the identity) (which we can do since there are necessarily 26 elements in $G$ besides the identity), and let $m = \ord{a}$ denote the order of $a$. By Corollary 7.9, we know that $m$ must be a divisor of 27, since $|G| = 27$. \\

	Thus, since the divisors of 27 are 1, 3, 9, and 27, we need only consider four possible cases for $m$.

	\begin{step}
		$m = 1$.
	\end{step}

	I lied, this isn't \emph{really} an actual case, as we will soon discover, but is a point needing addressing. Although 1 is indeed a divisor of 27, the order of $a$ cannot possibly be 1 because we defined $a$ to be any arbitrary element of $G$ \underline{besides} the identity. To see why this is the case, notice that if $m$ did in fact equal 1, we would have that $a^{m} = 1$, and so:

	\begin{align*}
		a^{m} &= 1 &&\text{(By definition of order)} \\
		\implies a^{1} &= 1 &&\text{(By assumption that $m = 1$)} \\
		\implies a &= 1 &&\text{(By definition of exponentiation)} \\
	\end{align*}

	Which would contradict $a$ as having been defined as any element of $G$ besides the identity. Hence $m$ cannot equal 1, leaving us only to consider the following three other cases.

	\begin{step}
		$m = 3$.
	\end{step}

	In the case where $m = 3$, then it immediately follows that $G$ contains an element of order 3 since $m$ is the order of $a$ by definition!

	\begin{step}
		$m = 9$.
	\end{step}

	Assuming now that $m = 9$, then by definition of order $m = 9$ is the smallest positive integer satisfying $a^{m} = 1$. So, letting $x \coloneq a^{3}$ (for reasons which will soon become apparent), we therefore have that:

	\begin{align*}
		x^{3} &= (a^{3})^{3} &&\text{(By definition of $x$)} \\
		&= a^{9} &&\text{(By exponent laws)} \\
		&= 1 &&\text{(By definition of order, and since $\ord{a} = m = 9$)} \\
	\end{align*}

	Now since $x$ and $x^{2}$ both cannot equal the identity (because if either of them did, then one of $a^{3}$ or $a^{6}$ would equal the identity, thereby contradicting our assumption that $\ord{a} = m = 9$), we have that $n = 3$ is the smallest positive integer satisfying $x^{3} = 1$. \\

	Thus, by definition of order, $\ord{x} = 3$, and since $x$ belongs to $G$ by virtue of the fact $G$'s a group and so must be closed under the group operation, $G$ contains an element of order 3. \\

	Therefore, since when $m = 3$, $G$ contains an element of order 3, we now need to consider the last possible case.

	\begin{step}
		$m = 27$.
	\end{step}

	Mimicking the approach we used for case \textbf{(III)}, assuming that $m = 27$ and letting $x \coloneq a^{9}$, we have that:

	\begin{align*}
		x^{3} &= (a^{3})^{3} &&\text{(By definition of $x$)} \\
		&= a^{27} &&\text{(By exponent laws)} \\
		&= 1 &&\text{(By definition of order, and since $\ord{a} = m = 27$)} \\
	\end{align*}

	This implies that the order of $x$ is 3 since, by the same logic argued before, $\ord{x}$ being any other number would necessarily contradict our assumption that $\ord{a} = m = 3$. \\

	Thus, since $x \in G$ is an element of order 3, $G$ contains an element of order 3 in this case as well. \\\\

	Therefore, since cases \textbf{(II)}, \textbf{(III)}, and \textbf{(IV)} all imply that $G$ has an element of order 3, and since case \textbf{(I)} cannot actually happen, $G$ \underline{must} have an element of order 3, and the statement is proven. \\
\end{solution}

\begin{subproblem}
	Let $G$ be a group of even order. Prove that $G$ has an odd number of elements of order $2$.
\end{subproblem}

\begin{solution}
	Let's let $J = \set{x \in G \colon \ord{x} = 2}$ denote the set of all elements of order 2 in $G$. Let's also let $m$ denote the cardinality of $J$, so that $m = |J|$. To prove the statement, we'll want to show that $m$ must be an odd number, which by definition of oddness, requires us to show that there exists some integer $k \in \mathbb{Z}$ such that $m = 2k - 1$.

	To do this, for reasons I soon hope to make clear, we will proceed by proving that the set $H = \set{1} \sqcup J$ constitutes a subgroup of $G$. Letting $x$ and $y$ be arbitrary elements of our newly defined set $H$ (which is clearly nonempty), so long as we can demonstrate that $xy^{-1} \in H$, we can appeal to the one-line subgroup test to immediately deduce that $H$ is a subgroup of $G$. To do so, however, requires establishing a few preliminaries first:

	\begin{step}
		$y^{-1} = y$.
	\end{step}

	The first preliminary we'll seek to prove is that $y^{-1} = y$ for all $y \in H$. \\

	Since $y \in H$, we know that $y$ must either be the identity element, or belong to $J$. \\

	If $y$ equals the identity, then clearly $y^{-1} = y$, since $(1)(1) = 1$ by definition of the identity. \\
	
	If $y$ belongs to $J$ however, then by definition of $J$, we have that $y$ must be an element of order $2$ in $G$. If this is the case, then by definition of order, we know $y^{2} = 1$, which allows us to conclude that:

	\begin{align*}
		y^{2} &= 1 &&\text{(By definition of order)} \\
		y^{-1}y^{2} &= y^{-1}(1) &&\text{(Multiplying both sides on the left by $y^{-1}$)} \\
		y^{-1}y^{2} &= y^{-1} &&\text{(By definition of the identity)} \\
		y^{1} &= y^{-1} &&\text{(By exponent laws)} \\
		y &= y^{-1} &&\text{(By definition of exponentiation)} \\
	\end{align*}

	Thus, when $y \in J$, $y^{-1} = y$ as well. So because, $y^{-1} = y$ in both possible cases, this preliminary is proven.

	\begin{step}
		$x^{2} = 1$.
	\end{step}

	The second small, yet crucial, preliminary we must prove is that $x^{2} = 1$ for all $x \in H$. \\

	Once again, since $x$ belongs to $H$, $x$ either equals the identity element or belongs to $J$. \\

	If $x$ is the identity, then since $(x)(x) = (1)(1) = 1$ by definition of the identity, we quickly can arrive at our desired conclusion that $x^{2} = 1$. \\
	
	The other case isn't any harder at all. If $x \in J$, then $\ord{x} = 2$ by definition of $J$, and by definition of $J$, and by definition of order, $x^{2} = 1$ as well. \\

	Thus, $x^{2} = 1$ for all $x \in H$, and since $x$ is arbitrary in $H$, this exact argument also applies to $y$, meaning $y^{2} = 1$. \\\\

	Now that these two preliminaries have been established, we can see by some simple algebra that:

	\begin{align*}
		(xy^{-1})^{2} &= (xy)^{2} &&\text{(Since $y^{-1} = y$, by preliminary \textbf{(I)})} \\
		&= y^{2}x^{2} &&\text{(By exponent laws)} \\
		&= (1)(1) &&\text{(Since $x^{2} = 1$ and $y^{2} = 1$ by preliminary \textbf{(II)})} \\
		&= 1 &&\text{(By definition of the identity)} \\
	\end{align*}
	
	Which tells us something incredibly important; namely that $1 \le \ord{xy^{-1}} \le 2$, since $1$ is the only positive integer less than 2, and because we've demonstrated that $(xy^{-1})^{2}$ always equals the identity. \\

	By our earlier logic, we know that if $\ord{xy^{-1}} = 1$, then $xy^{-1} = 1$ and if $\ord{xy^{-1}} = 2$, then $xy^{-1} \ in J$ by definition of $J$. And so, since $xy^{-1} \in \set{1} \sqcup J$, $xy^{-1} \in H$. Therefore, by the one-line subgroup test, $H$ is necessarily a subgroup of $G$. \\

	By \textbf{Corollary 7.9}, we know that if $H$ contains \underline{any} order two elements, then the order of $H$ must be divisible by 2; or in other words, $|H|$ must be even. Since $|H|$ is even in this case, there must exist some integer $k \in \mathbb{Z}$ such that $|H| = 2k$. But, since $H$ is the disjoint union of $\set{1}$ and $J$, we know that $|H| = |\set{1}| + |J| = 1 + m$ by definition of $m$. \\

	Putting this all together, we know that whenever $J$ is nonempty, then:

	\begin{align*}
		|H| &= 2k &&\text{(By characterization of $|H|$)} \\
		\implies 1 + m &= 2k &&\text{(By characterization of $|H|$)} \\
		\implies m &= 2k - 1 &&\text{(Solving for $m$)} \\
	\end{align*}

	Thus, whenever $H$ contains any elements of order $2$, the cardinality of $J$ must be an odd number, and because $J$ was defined to be the set of all elements of order 2 in $G$, $G$ must contain an odd number of order two elements in this case. \\

	But isn't it possible for $J$ to be empty? No. Recall from earlier that $G$ is a group of even order. So let $n \in \mathbb{Z}$ be an integer such that $|G| = 2n$. Since $G$ must then have at least two elements, we can always pick some element $g \in G$ such that $g \neq 1$. Letting $l = \ord{g}$, we know from earlier that $l \neq 1$ since $g \neq 1$. \\

	By \textbf{Corollary 7.9}, we also know $l$ must be a divisor of $2n$. Thus, we can now narrow our search down to two possible cases:

	\begin{step}
		$l$ is even.
	\end{step}
	

\end{solution}
